\begin{abstract}
An RR Lyrae star pulsates with a period of about half a day and a very stable, sharply shaped light curve, so it
can be used as a clock. If it orbits a companion, its distance from us changes periodically and the pulsation
signal arrives earlier or later by the light-travel time across the orbit --- minutes to tens of minutes for
companions of $0.1$--$1\,\Msun$ on 3--30-yr orbits. We measure this delay once per observing season for
17{,}488 LMC RRab stars with OGLE-II/III/IV photometry (1997--2016) and, for 6{,}612 of them, MACHO $B$ and $R$ photometry
(1992--1999). The resulting time series of delays is the \OC\ diagram. This note explains the measurement in
words and in equations, illustrates it with mock data, and documents the pipeline and its tests (\cref{sec:words,sec:eqs,sec:mocks,sec:pipeline}).
It then gives the results: the candidate selection (\cref{sec:selection}; 75 candidates, 11 in the cleanest tier); the calibration
on simulations (\cref{sec:partB}), which shows that the real stars carry red, mostly intrinsic timing noise that accounts for
the number of candidates and gives an upper limit $f<2.7\%$ on the fraction with $0.4$--$1.5\,\Msun$ companions at
$P_{\rm orb}=1$--10\,kd; a comparison with the Galactic-bulge candidates and a Gaia test (\cref{sec:hajdu}); and frozen
predictions for 2016--2026 that post-2016 OGLE-IV photometry would test decisively (\cref{sec:pred}).
\end{abstract}

\section{What we measure, in simple words}\label{sec:words}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The clock.} An RRab star brightens steeply (within about an hour) and fades slowly, repeating every
$P\approx0.5$--$0.7$\,d. The shape repeats almost exactly, so the times of the steep rise are sharp time markers.
\item \textbf{The prediction.} From the catalogue period and one reference epoch we predict when the rise should
happen in any later year: the ``calculated'' time $C$.
\item \textbf{The observation.} In each observing season we find the time shift that best lines up the observed light
curve with the star's own template: the ``observed'' time $O$. The difference $O-C$ is the \emph{delay} of that season.
\item \textbf{What changes the delay.} (i) A slightly wrong catalogue period makes $O-C$ grow linearly with time.
(ii) A slow, steady change of the period (stellar evolution) bends $O-C$ into a parabola.
(iii) An orbiting companion moves the RR Lyrae towards and away from us; the light has a little further or a little
less distance to travel, so $O-C$ oscillates with the orbital period (\cref{fig:orbit}). This is the
light-travel-time effect (LTTE).
(iv) The star itself can change its period irregularly (jumps, wander, Blazhko modulation); this also moves
$O-C$ and is the main confusion.
\item \textbf{Why it works at the LMC.} The delay depends only on the size of the orbit, not on the distance to the star.
A $0.6\,\Msun$ companion on a 3000-d orbit gives $\pm17.5$\,min everywhere in the Universe. The price of the LMC
distance is photometric noise: our stars are at $I\approx19$, so one season's delay is measured to about 4\,min (median).
\item \textbf{What we look for.} A delay that oscillates smoothly and repeats, with the same amplitude in every band and
survey, on top of an ordinary smooth period change; and, statistically, how many such stars there are.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What we measure, in equations}\label{sec:eqs}

\subsection{Light curve, ephemeris and delay}
Let $T(\phi)$ be the star's mean light curve (template) as a function of pulsation phase $\phi\in[0,1)$, written as a
Fourier series of order $K$ (we use $K=8$),
\begin{equation}
T(\phi)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[a_k\cos(2\pi k\phi)+b_k\sin(2\pi k\phi)\right].
\end{equation}
With a reference period $P$ and epoch $T_0$ (the OGLE-IV catalogue values), the observed magnitude is modelled as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lc}
m(t)=z_s+\delta m_j+\alpha_j\,T\!\left(\frac{t-\tau_j-T_0}{P}\right)+\varepsilon ,
\end{equation}
where $s$ labels the survey segment (OGLE-II, -III, -IV, MACHO; separate zero points $z_s$), $j$ the observing season,
$\tau_j$ the season's delay, $\delta m_j$ a mean-magnitude offset and $\alpha_j$ an amplitude factor (both nuisance
parameters; $\alpha_j$ is also a Blazhko diagnostic), and $\varepsilon$ the photometric noise. A single $\tau_j$ shifts all
harmonics together: this is what a change in light-travel time does. The set $\{\tau_j\}$ versus time is the
\OC\ diagram; $\tau>0$ means the pulsation arrives late.

\subsection{Ordinary period change: the quadratic}
If the period changes slowly and steadily, $P(t)=P_0+\Pdot\,t$, the $n$-th maximum occurs at
$T_n=T_0'+nP_0+\tfrac12\Pdot P_0 n^2$, and with $n\simeq t/P_0$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quad}
\tau(t)=c_0+c_1\,t+c_2\,t^2,\qquad c_1=\frac{P_0-P}{P},\qquad c_2=\frac{\Pdot}{2P}.
\end{equation}
$c_0$ is an uninteresting constant (epoch and template-phase convention), $c_1$ a period error, $c_2$ the rate of
period change. For an evolutionary $\Pdot=0.1$\,d\,Myr$^{-1}$ ($2.7\times10^{-10}$\,d\,d$^{-1}$) and $P=0.55$\,d,
$c_2=2.5\times10^{-10}$\,d$^{-1}$, i.e.\ $\tau\approx2.5\times10^{-10}\times(4000)^2$\,d $=0.004$\,d $\approx350$\,s
at 4000\,d from the reference epoch: comparable to the orbital signals, so the quadratic is always part of the model.

\subsection{Light-travel-time effect}
If the RR Lyrae (mass $M_1$) orbits a companion ($M_2$), the delay is its line-of-sight position divided by $c$
\citep{Irwin1952}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ltte}
\tau_{\rm LTTE}(t)=\frac{a_1\sin i}{c}\;\frac{1-e^2}{1+e\cos\nu(t)}\,\sin\!\big(\nu(t)+\omega\big),
\end{equation}
with true anomaly $\nu$ from Kepler's equation, eccentricity $e$, argument of periastron $\omega$, and
\begin{equation}
a_1=\left[\frac{G(M_1+M_2)P_{\rm orb}^2}{4\pi^2}\right]^{1/3}\frac{M_2}{M_1+M_2}.
\end{equation}
From the fitted $P_{\rm orb}$ and semi-amplitude $A\equiv a_1\sin i/c$ one obtains the mass function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fm}
f(M_2)=\frac{4\pi^2(cA)^3}{G P_{\rm orb}^2}=\frac{(M_2\sin i)^3}{(M_1+M_2)^2},
\end{equation}
and the primary's radial-velocity semi-amplitude $K_1=2\pi c A/[P_{\rm orb}\sqrt{1-e^2}]$.
The full \OC\ model is $\tau(t)=c_0+c_1t+c_2t^2+\tau_{\rm LTTE}(t)$.

\subsection{Measuring one season's delay}
For fixed template $(a_k,b_k,z_s)$, each season's $(\tau_j,\delta m_j,\alpha_j)$ is fitted by weighted least squares
(Gauss--Newton after a coarse search over one full cycle). Its Fisher (Cram\'er--Rao) error is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fisher}
\sigma_{\tau_j}^{-2}\simeq\sum_{i\in j}\frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}\left(\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}\right)_i^2
=\sum_{i\in j}\frac{\alpha_j^2\,T'(\phi_i)^2}{P^2\sigma_i^2},
\end{equation}
scaled by $\sqrt{\max(\chi^2_\nu,1)}$. The steep rising branch dominates the sum. Template and delays are iterated to
convergence. Because a constant delay is degenerate with the template phase, we fix the gauge so that the
template's fundamental harmonic has phase zero.

\subsection{Detection statistic $D$}
On the season delays $\{t_j,\tau_j,\sigma_j\}$ we compare two Gaussian models with variance $\sigma_j^2+s^2$
(extra jitter $s$ fitted in each):
$H_0$: \cref{eq:quad} (+ MACHO $B$ and $R$ offsets with band-lag priors, \cref{sec:tie}); $H_1$: $H_0$ + $A_s\sin(2\pi t/P_{\rm orb})+A_c\cos(2\pi t/P_{\rm orb})$.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D}
D=2\max_{P_{\rm orb}}\left[\ln\mathcal L_1(P_{\rm orb})-\ln\mathcal L_0\right].
\end{equation}
At fixed $P_{\rm orb}$ and for white noise $D\sim\chi^2_2$; for $N$ seasons of error $\sigma$ and amplitude $A$,
$D\approx NA^2/(2\sigma^2)$. $D$ tests an orbit against a \emph{smooth} period change plus white noise; it does
not by itself reject irregular intrinsic period changes (\cref{sec:nuis}).

\section{The pipeline}\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Data}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{OGLE-IV} LMC RR Lyrae collection \citep{Soszynski2016}: 41{,}471 stars; $I$-band light curves for 41{,}209,
epochs HJD$'$ = 5260--8924, where HJD$'$ = HJD $-$ 2450000 (for 95\% of the stars the public data end at HJD$'\approx7516$, mid-2016).
\url{https://www.astrouw.edu.pl/ogle/ogle4/OCVS/lmc/rrlyr/}
\item \textbf{OGLE-III} \citep{Soszynski2009}: 24{,}906 light curves, HJD$'=2166$--$4540$ (2001--2008); the 2024
re-issue of the files includes \textbf{OGLE-II} epochs (from HJD$'=447$, 1997) for 7{,}784 stars.
Same star numbering as OGLE-IV (24{,}818 matches, median separation $0.05''$).
\url{https://www.astrouw.edu.pl/ogle/ogle3/OIII-CVS/lmc/rrlyr/}
\item \textbf{MACHO} $B$ and $R$ photometry (1992--1999) via the NCI TAP service
(\url{https://machotap.asvo.nci.org.au/ncitap/tap}), queried per tile for the RR Lyrae only (MACHO IDs from the
OGLE-III cross-identification): 8{,}406 of 8{,}557 requested stars returned. Both bands are used.
\item \textbf{Sample:} 17{,}488 RRab with a successful OGLE $I$ timing fit; 6{,}612 of them with MACHO
(6{,}599 with both $B$ and $R$), 10{,}876 OGLE only.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Time system}
OGLE times are HJD (UTC). MACHO \texttt{dateobs} is MJD and is converted to HJD for each star (observer at Mount
Stromlo); towards the LMC, near the south ecliptic pole, the heliocentric correction is only $\pm40$\,s
(tested). MACHO time stamps refer to the start of the exposure ($+150$\,s to mid-exposure; \citealt{Michalska2005}); a constant offset of this kind is absorbed by the per-band
MACHO offset (\cref{sec:tie}).

\subsection{Seasons}
A season is one observing year, with the boundary at HJD$'$ mod 365.25 $=245$ (the middle of the OGLE seasonal gap).
MACHO observed the LMC nearly year-round, so gap-based seasons would merge years. Seasons with fewer than 15 epochs are dropped.

\subsection{Timing fit per star and band}
\cref{eq:lc} with $K=8$, one template per band (OGLE $I$, MACHO $B$, MACHO $R$), separate zero points per survey segment,
per-season $(\tau_j,\delta m_j,\alpha_j)$, $4\sigma$ clipping, template$\leftrightarrow$delay iterations until the mask is
stable and $\max|\Delta\tau|<10^{-7}$\,d, the fundamental-phase gauge, and errors from \cref{eq:fisher} scaled by
$\sqrt{\max(\chi^2_\nu,1)}$. Each season also yields a \emph{harmonic coherence}: the delay measured from the fundamental
minus that measured from the higher harmonics. For a pure time shift (LTTE) it is zero within errors; a change of light-curve
shape makes it non-zero. Delays are defined modulo $P$; they are unwrapped between consecutive seasons, and a slip is
repaired only if the repair reduces the jumps to both adjacent seasons (a more aggressive rule made noisy stars worse).
\emph{Measured precision:} median season error 189\,s for MACHO stars and 270\,s for OGLE-only stars (combined over bands),
strongly amplitude-dependent.

\subsection{Tying MACHO to OGLE}\label{sec:tie}
Band-dependent template shapes leave a constant offset $\Delta$ between each MACHO delay series and the OGLE $I$ series
(\cref{fig:bands}). The same behaviour is found between OGLE's own $V$ and $I$, which share one time system: the offset
is a physical band lag, not a clock error. Calibrated on the full sample (\texttt{scripts/calibrate\_band\_lag.py}):
\begin{equation}
\Delta_B=-6648\,P+1966\ {\rm s}\ (\sigma_{\rm int}=293\,{\rm s},\ N=1582),\qquad
\Delta_R=-2656\,P+718\ {\rm s}\ (\sigma_{\rm int}=218\,{\rm s},\ N=1552),
\end{equation}
with $P$ in days ($-0.036$ and $-0.016$ cycles). In the \OC\ fits each MACHO band has its own offset with this relation as a
Gaussian prior (pseudo-observations, identical under $H_0$ and $H_1$). About 13\% of MACHO stars had a band series a whole
pulsation cycle away from the prior after unwrapping; these are shifted by the integer number of cycles that best matches
the prior (\texttt{oc.align\_bands}). Simulated stars draw their MACHO offsets from the same relation and scatter.

\subsection{Common-mode timing correction}
Stacking all stars' \OC\ residuals by (band, year) reveals a common pattern. It is solved iteratively as a clipped mean,
with the directions that are degenerate with each star's own model (a common quadratic and per-band constants) projected
out; candidates are excluded. The MACHO $B$ and $R$ patterns agree within a few seconds (1992--1999, $B$: $-37$, $+26$, $+34$,
$+39$, $+56$, $+53$, $-60$, $-110$\,s; $R$: $-38$, $+24$, $+32$, $+41$, $+55$, $+49$, $-63$, $-99$\,s), so this is a timing
systematic of the MACHO data; the OGLE pattern spans $-66$ to $+46$\,s. A null test (white-noise delays at the real epochs) is
consistent with zero. The correction is subtracted from every star.

\subsection{Orbit search and the annual-sampling limit}\label{sec:nyquist}
$D$ (\cref{eq:D}) is maximized over trial periods uniform in frequency, from 400\,d to twice the baseline; $H_1$ is fitted both
with a circular orbit (one harmonic) and with two harmonics (eccentric orbits). Season delays sample the \OC\ about once per
year, but the epochs of the seasons scatter by 40\,d rms within the year; the spectral window at $1/{\rm yr}$ is 0.85, not 1, and
injected orbits from 300\,d upwards are recovered at the true period in 86--100\% of cases (\texttt{scripts/alias\_test.py}).
The real short-period limit is the averaging of the orbit over a season of $\approx240$\,d, which reduces the amplitude by
$|\mathrm{sinc}(\pi\,240/P_{\rm orb})|=0.23$, 0.50, 0.66 at 300, 400, 500\,d; hence the grid starts at 400\,d. An alias flag
records the difference in $D$ between the best period and its $\pm1/{\rm yr}$ aliases.

\subsection{Noise models, nuisances and discriminators}\label{sec:nuis}
Simulations use each star's real cadence and errors and its own fitted templates (\texttt{src/rrlbin/simulate.py}); one
delay realization is shared by all bands. Classes: white noise (null); Blazhko (coherent phase and amplitude modulation,
$P_B=20$--3000\,d); abrupt period changes, small and large; random-walk phase, small and large; LTTE (log-uniform
$P_{\rm orb}=400$--$10^4$\,d, $M_2=0.05$--$1.5\,\Msun$, isotropic, half eccentric); and an \textbf{empirical} class that
reproduces the real stars' excess timing noise (below).

\textbf{The real excess timing noise is red.} Real stars scatter about $H_0$ far more than the photometric errors allow
($\chi^2_\nu$ p50/p90 $\approx2/30$, against $\approx1/2$ for white-noise simulations, which do reproduce the real per-season
errors to within 2--3\%). The excess is common to the bands: same-season residuals correlate with Spearman $\rho=0.58$ between
MACHO $B$ and $R$ and $\rho=0.37$ between MACHO and OGLE (nulls: $\approx0$). It is mostly intrinsic to the stars. It is also
\emph{smooth}: per survey, the scatter about a quadratic is much smaller than over the joint baseline, whereas a Brownian random
walk overpredicts it by a factor $\sim7$--10. We model it as a Gaussian process (GP) wander plus white jitter,
\begin{equation}
y_j=\mathbf x_j^{\rm T}\bm\beta+w(t_j)+\epsilon_j,\quad
\langle w(t)w(t')\rangle=A_w^2\exp\!\left[-\frac{(t-t')^2}{2\ell^2}\right],\quad \epsilon_j\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_j^2+s^2),
\end{equation}
fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML; ML biases $A_w$ low) on a grid $\ell\in\{700,1500,3000,6000\}$\,d
(\texttt{oc.gp\_null}). Real fits give $A_w/\sigma$ p50/p75/p90 = 2.1/11/22 (MACHO stars) and 1.5/7.8/22 (OGLE-only);
$\ell=700$\,d is the most common. The \textbf{empirical} class draws $(s/\sigma,A_w/\sigma,\ell)$ from the real fits
(candidates excluded) and splits the white jitter 64\% common to all bands and 36\% per instrument (MACHO $B+R$ shared), which
reproduces the measured band correlations ($\rho_{BR}=0.63$, $\rho_{\rm MO}=0.37$). The per-band $\chi^2_\nu$ of the empirical
simulations matches the real stars within a factor 1.5--2.5, with the simulations slightly noisier: conservative for false alarms.

\textbf{Discriminators} (computed identically for data and simulations):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item amplitude constancy $\chi^2_\nu(\alpha_j)<2$, pooled over bands (Blazhko veto; LTTE does not change amplitude);
\item harmonic coherence $\chi^2_\nu$ (shape changes; LTTE shifts all harmonics equally);
\item at least 1.5 orbital cycles within the baseline;
\item a physical ceiling: $A$ below $a_1/c$ for $M_2=2\,\Msun$ at the fitted period;
\item for MACHO stars, a predictive test: the orbit period and parameters are found from the seasons after HJD$'=450$ only
(unwrapping and band alignment also done on these training seasons), then the held-out 1992--1996 MACHO delays are predicted;
the score is the log-likelihood gain of the orbit prediction over the $H_0$ prediction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Code and tests}
Package \texttt{rrlbin}: \texttt{io}, \texttt{timing} (per-season fits), \texttt{pipeline} (Level 1: star $\to$ delay series),
\texttt{oc} (Level 2: unwrapping, band alignment, common mode, orbit search, GP noise model, predictive test; shared by data
and simulations), \texttt{ltte}, \texttt{simulate}, \texttt{kepler\_fit} (Keplerian fits), \texttt{predict} (kriging predictions),
\texttt{mixture}. 40 unit tests (\texttt{pytest}, all pass), including recovery of noiseless delays, gauge invariance, pull
distributions, the Kepler solver, the null calibration of $D$, the alias test, leakage-free predictive scores, band alignment, GP
recovery ($A_w$ 950 vs 900\,s injected at $\ell=1500$\,d), Keplerian recovery of $e=0$, 0.4, 0.7 orbits and the kriging limits.
Two bugs found by tests in v3 (amplitudes 19\% low from a parameter-name collision; prediction leakage) are fixed with
regression tests. Long runs use a chunked, resumable runner with a manifest check. Full log: \texttt{JOURNAL.md}.

\subsection{The panels}\label{sec:panels}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,style=nextline]
\item[(a) Detection statistic.] Distribution of $D$ (\cref{eq:D}) for real stars (grey) and four simulated classes.
White-noise nulls (black; a constant \Pdot\ and photometric noise only) never exceed $D=23.0$ in 2{,}998 simulations; their
per-star false-alarm thresholds are $D=13.8$ (5\%), 16.9 (1\%) and 20.5 (0.1\%). Real stars have a long tail far beyond
the nulls: 1{,}009 (5.8\%) have $D>40$, where white noise predicts none. Simulated orbits (blue), Blazhko modulation (orange)
and large abrupt period changes (green) all populate that tail, so $D$ alone cannot separate orbits from intrinsic period
changes. (The empirical class, which reproduces the real tail, is shown in \cref{fig:partB}a.)
\item[(b) $D$ versus best period.] Each star's $D$ at the period that maximizes it. The vertical banding is the discreteness
of the frequency grid (nearly all MACHO stars share one baseline and hence the same grid points). Candidates (red; 81\% of
them) concentrate at 2{,}500--5{,}000\,d, where a 24-yr baseline covers about 2--4 cycles and the search is most sensitive.
\item[(c) Amplitude versus period.] Fitted semi-amplitude $A=a_1\sin i/c$ with the edge-on LTTE amplitude for

period changes far larger than any companion of $\le2\,\Msun$ can produce. Candidates lie between the $0.15$ and

Cut 3 (dashed) removes signals that are not resolved season by season.

shifts the light curve without changing its amplitude ($\chi^2_\nu\approx1$), whereas Blazhko modulation changes it:
the median $\chi^2_\nu$ is 0.97 for simulated orbits and 0.98 for nulls, but 1.9 for simulated Blazhko stars and 1.46
for real stars. The black histogram (real, $D>40$) is shifted to large $\chi^2_\nu$: most high-$D$ real stars change
amplitude from season to season.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{$D>40$.} With 17{,}488 stars, fewer than one white-noise false alarm requires a per-star false-alarm
probability below $\sim6\times10^{-5}$, i.e.\ well beyond the largest null value ($D=23$ in 2{,}998 simulations).
$D>40$ is chosen conservatively high because the real contaminants are not white noise; lowering it mainly admits
more intrinsic period changes (panel a).
\item \textbf{Amplitude constancy, $\chi^2_\nu(\alpha)<2$.} Motivated by the physics: the LTTE is a pure time shift
(\cref{fig:lc}), so any season-to-season amplitude change points to Blazhko modulation (or changing blends). The cut
removes 79\% of simulated Blazhko stars that passed cut 1 (0.316 $\to$ 0.065) while keeping 99\% of the orbits
(0.304 $\to$ 0.302), and 64\% of real stars with $D>40$. It removes \emph{no} empirical-noise simulations, because that class
has no amplitude modulation, whereas it removes most real high-$D$ stars; this matters for the contamination estimate (\cref{sec:partB}).
\item \textbf{$A/\sigma_{\rm season}>3$.} The orbital amplitude must be large compared with a single season's error, so
that the curve is seen season by season and is not only an integrated excess; it mainly guards against
low-amplitude, high-jitter solutions.
\item \textbf{At least 1.5 orbital cycles.} The decisive cut against intrinsic period changes: a sinusoid fitted over
less than about one cycle mimics a smooth trend. It removes 86\% of the large abrupt period changes (0.778 $\to$ 0.109),
55\% of the large random walks and 58\% of the empirical noise, at the cost of a third of the simulated orbits: those with
periods longer than about 2/3 of the baseline, which cannot be confirmed as periodic anyway.
\item \textbf{Physical ceiling.} The fitted amplitude must be achievable with $M_2\le2\,\Msun$ at the fitted period
(panel c). At this stage it removes no simulated orbits but 2/3 of the remaining large random walks (0.073 $\to$ 0.025)
and 17 real stars. The cut is applied identically to data and simulations.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent The cuts were set on physical grounds and on the simulations, not tuned on the real candidates. They are not
a clean binary selection: large abrupt period changes still pass at 9\%, the empirical noise at 2.6\% and Blazhko at 2.2\%
(\cref{tab:cuts}). How many of the real candidates are expected from noise alone is the subject of \cref{sec:partB}.

\subsection{The empirical noise reproduces the real stars}
Among MACHO stars, the fraction with $D$ above 10, 20, 40, 80 is 0.752, 0.327, 0.123, 0.017 for the real stars and
0.783, 0.325, 0.105, 0.015 for the empirical-noise simulations; white noise gives 0.243, 0.002, 0, 0 (\cref{fig:partB}a).
The whole high-$D$ tail of the real population, including the region of the candidates, is therefore what the measured
intrinsic timing noise produces. Pure noise (null) never passes the cuts.

\subsection{How many candidates does noise alone produce?}
The empirical class has no amplitude modulation, so it passes the amplitude veto (cut~2) almost always, whereas only 46\% of
the real stars that pass the other four cuts also pass it. Real timing noise often comes with Blazhko-like amplitude
changes. The comparison is therefore made in two ways (\cref{fig:partB}c):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{All cuts except the amplitude veto} (where the empirical class is a valid background model): 150 real MACHO
stars against $171\pm13$ expected from noise ($0.0259\times6612$, Poisson error).
\item \textbf{All cuts, the veto corrected} with the real pass fraction (0.46): 69 real candidates against $\approx79\pm9$.
\item \textbf{Tier-1 proxy} (all cuts, $\chi^2_\nu(\alpha)<1.5$, coherence $\chi^2_\nu<1.5$, predictive score $>2$; the
Keplerian-fit and crowding criteria of Part C cannot be applied to simulations), veto corrected with the real pass fraction
of this stricter cut (0.33): 17 real against $\approx24\pm5$.
\end{itemize}
In none of these is there an excess of orbit-like signals over the timing noise. The OGLE-only sample (10{,}876 stars) has 6
candidates against 14 expected (95\% upper bound 31), and no star passes the Tier-1 proxy (OGLE-only stars have no predictive test).

\textbf{Caveat (circularity).} The empirical noise is drawn from the REML noise fits of the real stars (candidates excluded).
Undetected orbits in the other stars are absorbed into those fits, so the noise model can contain some orbital signal. This
makes the background estimate conservative for detection, but it means that the population comparison cannot by itself prove
that \emph{no} candidate is a binary. The veto correction assumes that binaries and noise pass the amplitude veto at the same
rate in the real data; a planned refinement adds measured amplitude modulation to the empirical class so that the veto is
calibrated directly.

\subsection{Completeness}
For injected orbits (MACHO stars, isotropic, half eccentric; \cref{fig:partB}d), the cuts recover $M_2=0.4$--$1.5\,\Msun$
companions at 11\% (400--1000\,d), 56\% (1--3\,kd) and 41\% (3--10\,kd); $M_2=0.15$--$0.4\,\Msun$ at 0.5\%, 10\% and 15\%; and
$M_2<0.15\,\Msun$ at $\le2\%$. For OGLE-only stars (baseline 1997/2001--2016) the completeness is far lower: 2\%, 13\% and 2\%
for $M_2=0.4$--$1.5\,\Msun$, because of the shorter baseline and the 1.5-cycle cut.

\subsection{Upper limits on the binary fraction}
For $M_2=0.4$--$1.5\,\Msun$ and $P_{\rm orb}=1$--10\,kd (completeness 0.478 for MACHO stars):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Model-free} (every candidate treated as a binary; 95\% Poisson upper limit on 69 is 84.3):
$f<84.3/(6612\times0.478)=2.7\%$ for MACHO stars and $f<1.5\%$ for OGLE-only stars (6 candidates, low completeness).
For $M_2=0.15$--$0.4\,\Msun$: $f<9.9\%$ (MACHO) and $<11\%$ (OGLE-only).
\item \textbf{Background-subtracted} (150 stars before the amplitude veto, background $b=171$ with a Gaussian systematic
uncertainty, flat prior on the signal, Poisson likelihood): $f<1.4\%$, 2.4\% and 4.1\% for a 15\%, 25\% and 50\%
systematic on $b$. These depend on the noise model and are quoted as a range.
\end{itemize}
The binary fraction of LMC RRab with massive companions on 3--30-yr orbits is therefore at most a few per cent.
\textbf{Context.} The rotating, disc-like RR Lyrae of the Milky Way behave like a young, metal-rich thin-disc population
\citep{Iorio2021}; the most metal-rich ones ([Fe/H] $>-0.5$) have kinematic ages of $\sim$6--7\,Gyr \citep{Zhang2025}. Binary
evolution can produce such stars: partial envelope stripping on the red-giant branch places them in the instability strip, and
all metal-rich RR Lyrae are then predicted to have an A-, F-, G- or K-type companion with $P_{\rm orb}>1000$\,d
\citep{Bobrick2024}, typically $\sim$900--2000\,d \citep{Iorio2026}. Gaia DR3 astrometry shows some tension with a high hidden
binary fraction \citep{Iorio2026}. Our search is most complete in this regime of period and companion mass (56\% at 1--3\,kd for
$M_2=0.4$--$1.5\,\Msun$). The LMC RRab are, however, dominated by old, metal-poor stars, so these limits apply to the population as
a whole and do not test the metal-rich binary channel directly.

\textbf{Implication.} Population statistics give a limit, not a detection. Whether any individual candidate is a binary can
only be decided with more data: further orbital cycles (OGLE-IV after 2016, \cref{sec:pred}), Gaia DR4 photometry
(\cref{sec:hajdu}) and radial velocities.

\subsection{The bulge sample of Hajdu et al.\ (2021)}
\citet{Hajdu2021} examined $\sim$27{,}500 OGLE-III/IV O$-$C diagrams of bulge RRab (data to 2019; mean/median baselines 10.5/7.4\,yr),
pre-selected $\sim$400 by eye, and retained 87 after MCMC LTTE fits including a period-change term, in three quality classes
(Q1: 25, $P_{\rm orb}$ uncertainty $<1\%$; Q2: 32; Q3: 30, problematic fits). Their Table 1 was parsed from the arXiv source
(\texttt{scripts/parse\_hajdu2021.py}).

\textbf{Follow-up status.} We found no published confirmation or refutation of any of the 87. A 2026 study co-authored by Hajdu
and Prudil states that ``only a single RRL is confirmed as belonging to a binary system'' \citep{Salinas2026}, namely
TU~UMa, a field star \citep{Liska2016}. Radial-velocity tests of Galactic field timing candidates have been largely negative
(15 of 19 without binarity, \citealt{Barnes2021}; KIC~2831097 ruled out, \citealt{Poretti2025}). The status of the bulge
candidates is therefore \emph{untested}, not refuted. (Literature search: \texttt{docs/reviews/hajdu\_followup.md}; an ADS
full-text query could not be run.)

\paragraph{Where the bulge sample is stronger: precision.} At $I\approx15$--16 the bulge stars are 3--4\,mag brighter; their
orbital parameters are 2--4 times more precise ($\sigma_A/A$ 1.7\% vs 6.7\%, $\sigma_P/P$ 0.5\% vs 1.1\%, $\sigma_e$ 0.02 vs 0.10
for Q1 vs Tier 1). Their seasons were also split into segments (more O$-$C points). Their posterior widths assume white timing
noise beyond a constant $\dot P$, which understates the uncertainty if the period wanders (\cref{sec:nuis}).

\paragraph{What our sample adds, and what it does not.}
The main way an RR~Lyrae imitates an orbit is intrinsic phase wander (irregular period changes are common in every population
studied; e.g.\ \citealt{Alcock2000} for LMC RRc). Our Tier~1 differ from the bulge candidates as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Independent instruments} (MACHO $B$, $R$ and OGLE $I$). This rules out \emph{instrumental} artefacts (time stamps,
photometric systematics), but \emph{not} intrinsic wander, which every telescope sees identically (the same-season timing residuals
of MACHO and OGLE are correlated, $\rho=0.37$, \cref{sec:nuis}).
\item \textbf{More cycles} (median 2.9 vs 1.9). This helps, but a smooth wander with a correlation time of a few years still
produces one or two bumps over 24\,yr, which a sinusoid plus a quadratic fits.
\item \textbf{An out-of-sample prediction passed} (1992--1996 MACHO seasons). A partial safeguard only: smooth wander can continue
smoothly into the held-out years (simulated wander passes the full Tier-1 proxy $\sim$1\% of the time).
\item \textbf{Calibrated contamination}: simulations with a noise model matched to the real stars. This is what shows that the
candidate \emph{number} is consistent with wander alone (Part B, \cref{sec:partB}); the bulge analysis has no such calibration.
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{Where they are alike.} Our sensitivity limit ($A\gtrsim350$\,s) restricts us to the high-amplitude regime of the
bulge Q1 class: medians $A\approx800$\,s, $M_{2,\min}\approx0.45$--$0.5\,\Msun$, $K_1\approx6\,\kms$ in both
(\cref{fig:hajdu}a, f). We cannot test the low-mass Q2/Q3 population ($M_{2,\min}\sim0.1$--$0.2\,\Msun$).

\paragraph{Expectation.} The v3 simulations (Part B) show that the number of our candidates is consistent with the tail of the
real stars' own timing noise (69 observed vs $\approx79$ expected from noise at the candidate cuts; 17 vs $\approx24$ for a Tier-1 proxy; \cref{sec:partB}).
Neither sample therefore establishes binarity statistically; the same is likely true of the bulge sample, whose selection was not
calibrated against intrinsic wander. What separates an orbit from wander is strict repetition over more cycles, i.e.\ more data. Confirmation is, however, harder: at $V\approx19.4$,
measuring $K_1\sim6\,\kms$ requires $\sim$1--2\,\kms\ per epoch after removing a $\sim$60\,\kms\ pulsation curve
(published LMC RR~Lyrae RVs reach 10--35\,\kms\ per epoch).

\subsection{A photometric test with Gaia}
Gaia DR3 epoch $G$ photometry (2014.6--2017.4) exists for 62 of the 75 candidates (median 34 usable transits, per-transit
S/N $\approx59$). We fitted a $G$-band template and two delays, one per half of the DR3 window; the unknown $G$--$I$ band lag
cancels in their difference $\Delta\tau$, which is compared with each Keplerian prediction.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Calibration on 228 quiet non-candidate stars} (Gaia $\Delta\tau$ vs OGLE's own $\Delta\tau$ at the same epochs, OGLE
errors included): robust scatter of $z$ = 1.64 (2.2\% beyond $5\sigma$) $\Rightarrow$ Gaia errors inflated by 1.64.
\item \textbf{DR3 result}: only 4 of 53 testable candidates are informative ($|\Delta\tau_{\rm pred}|>2\sigma$). Two agree
(16755, Tier~1, $z=0.8$; 13469, $z=0.3$); two disagree (13392, Tier~2, $z=-6.7$; 01106, Tier~3, $z=+3.5$), versus $\sim$0.5 expected
by chance from the control tail. DR3 is too sparse for most orbits.
\item \textbf{DR4 forecast} (expected December 2026; 2014.6--2020.1, \emph{assumed} $\sim$2$\times$ the DR3 epochs; bins 2014.6--2017.4
and 2017.4--2020.1, the latter entirely after the public OGLE-IV data): testable at $>2\sigma$ for 6 of 9 Tier~1 (5 at $>3\sigma$),
8 of 13 Tier~2, 13 of 31 Tier~3. DR4 can therefore confirm or reject most of the strongest candidates photometrically, at no
observing cost. OGLE-IV photometry after 2016 (not public) would be a stronger test still (\cref{sec:pred}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Frozen predictions and the OGLE data after 2016}\label{sec:pred}

\subsection{OGLE kept observing the LMC}
The public OCVS light curves of the original catalogue stars end at HJD$'\approx7516$ (2016.4). OGLE-IV observed the LMC
continuously until March 2020, resumed in August 2022 and is still operating; the catalogue extensions of 2017/2019 (1{,}432
stars) carry public data to 2020.2 and show 26--110 epochs per season in our candidates' fields
(\texttt{scripts/ogle\_post2016\_cadence.py}). A high-cadence survey of the central LMC fields ran from October 2022 to May 2024
\citep{Mroz2024}. Its field list (LMC502, 503, 509, 510, 516) is from an agent's reading of the paper and has not been checked.
These fields host 49 of the 75 candidates, including 7 of the 11 Tier-1 stars. At $\sim$2400 epochs per season the expected
delay error is $132\,{\rm s}\times\sqrt{54/2400}\approx20$\,s per season, against 132\,s now. These data are available only on
request from the OGLE team; a request for all LMC OCVS RR Lyrae has been drafted (\texttt{docs/ogle\_request\_email\_short.md}).

\subsection{What is frozen}
Before any new data are seen, the predicted OGLE $I$ delays for 2016.4--2026.5 have been computed for the 28 Tier-1/2
candidates from the public data alone (\texttt{scripts/freeze\_predictions.py}, \texttt{src/rrlbin/predict.py}), under both hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item $H_1$: the best Keplerian orbit, plus the smooth-wander GP refitted to the orbit-subtracted delays;
\item $H_0$: no orbit, with the star's own REML GP noise model (\cref{sec:nuis}).
\end{itemize}
Each is a universal-kriging prediction (GP conditional mean and covariance, including the uncertainty of the quadratic and
MACHO offsets); the full covariances are stored. A residual bootstrap of the Keplerian fit (200 refits) is stored as well. Note
that the $H_1$ kriging covariance is conditional on the best-fit orbit, so the bootstrap envelope should be added when new
data are compared. The table \texttt{results/predictions/predictions\_2026-10-01.parquet} has SHA-256
\texttt{a79bc756\ldots607813d}, recorded with the code commit in \texttt{\_meta.json}, and is tagged
\texttt{predictions-2026-10-01} in the repository. The predictive standard deviations (medians) are 179\,s (2020) and 282\,s
(2024) under $H_1$, and 1.6\,ks and 2.8\,ks under $H_0$ (\cref{fig:pred}).

\section{Summary and next steps}\label{sec:summary}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item We measured per-season pulsation delays for 17{,}488 LMC RRab (OGLE-II/III/IV 1997--2016; MACHO $B$, $R$ 1992--1999 for
6{,}612) and searched the \OC\ diagrams for light-travel-time orbits.
\item 75 stars pass the candidate cuts (11 Tier 1, 17 Tier 2). The Tier-1 orbits have $P_{\rm orb}=5.4$--14.5\,yr, $A=350$--1380\,s
and $M_{2,\min}=0.14$--$0.96\,\Msun$.
\item The real stars carry red, mostly intrinsic timing noise. Simulations with that noise reproduce the real $D$ distribution
and predict as many candidates as are observed: there is no population excess. The binary fraction for
$M_2=0.4$--$1.5\,\Msun$, $P_{\rm orb}=1$--10\,kd is below 2.7\% (model-free; 1.4--4.1\% background-subtracted).
\item Individual candidates are untested, like the bulge candidates of \citet{Hajdu2021}. Predictions for 2016--2026 are frozen;
OGLE-IV data after 2016 would decide every Tier-1/2 candidate, and Gaia DR4 most Tier-1 ones.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Next steps.} (1) Add amplitude modulation, drawn from the real stars' $\alpha_j$ variability, to the empirical noise class,
and re-run the Part B comparison with the amplitude veto calibrated directly. (2) Obtain the post-2016 OGLE-IV photometry and
test the frozen predictions; re-run the full analysis on the 1992--2026 baseline. (3) Test the Tier-1 candidates with Gaia DR4
epoch photometry. Season-level outlier rejection in Level 2 is a minor open item (e.g.\ one bad MACHO $R$ season of 13854).
