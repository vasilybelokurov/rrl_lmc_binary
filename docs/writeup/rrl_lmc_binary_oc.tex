\begin{abstract}
An RR Lyrae star pulsates with a period of about half a day and a very stable, sharply shaped light curve, so it
can be used as a clock. If it orbits a companion, its distance from us changes periodically and the pulsation
signal arrives earlier or later by the light-travel time across the orbit --- minutes to tens of minutes for
companions of $0.1$--$1\,\Msun$ on 3--30-yr orbits. We measure this delay once per observing season for
17{,}492 LMC RRab stars with OGLE-II/III/IV photometry (1997--2016) and, for 6{,}614 of them, MACHO photometry
(1992--1999). The resulting time series of delays is the \OC\ diagram. This note explains the measurement in
words and in equations, illustrates it with mock data, and documents the pipeline, its tests and its known
limitations. The candidate selection is explained panel by panel in \cref{sec:selection}; results are summarized briefly in
\cref{sec:status}; the candidates are compared with the Galactic-bulge sample, and tested with Gaia photometry, in \cref{sec:hajdu}.
\end{abstract}

\section{What we measure, in simple words}\label{sec:words}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The clock.} An RRab star brightens steeply (within about an hour) and fades slowly, repeating every
$P\approx0.5$--$0.7$\,d. The shape repeats almost exactly, so the times of the steep rise are sharp time markers.
\item \textbf{The prediction.} From the catalogue period and one reference epoch we predict when the rise should
happen in any later year: the ``calculated'' time $C$.
\item \textbf{The observation.} In each observing season we find the time shift that best lines up the observed light
curve with the star's own template: the ``observed'' time $O$. The difference $O-C$ is the \emph{delay} of that season.
\item \textbf{What changes the delay.} (i) A slightly wrong catalogue period makes $O-C$ grow linearly with time.
(ii) A slow, steady change of the period (stellar evolution) bends $O-C$ into a parabola.
(iii) An orbiting companion moves the RR Lyrae towards and away from us; the light has a little further or a little
less distance to travel, so $O-C$ oscillates with the orbital period (\cref{fig:orbit}). This is the
light-travel-time effect (LTTE).
(iv) The star itself can change its period irregularly (jumps, wander, Blazhko modulation); this also moves
$O-C$ and is the main confusion.
\item \textbf{Why it works at the LMC.} The delay depends only on the size of the orbit, not on the distance to the star.
A $0.6\,\Msun$ companion on a 3000-d orbit gives $\pm17.5$\,min everywhere in the Universe. The price of the LMC
distance is photometric noise: our stars are at $I\approx19$, so one season's delay is measured to about 4\,min (median).
\item \textbf{What we look for.} A delay that oscillates smoothly and repeats, with the same amplitude in every band and
survey, on top of an ordinary smooth period change; and, statistically, how many such stars there are.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What we measure, in equations}\label{sec:eqs}

\subsection{Light curve, ephemeris and delay}
Let $T(\phi)$ be the star's mean light curve (template) as a function of pulsation phase $\phi\in[0,1)$, written as a
Fourier series of order $K$ (we use $K=8$),
\begin{equation}
T(\phi)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[a_k\cos(2\pi k\phi)+b_k\sin(2\pi k\phi)\right].
\end{equation}
With a reference period $P$ and epoch $T_0$ (the OGLE-IV catalogue values), the observed magnitude is modelled as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lc}
m(t)=z_s+\delta m_j+\alpha_j\,T\!\left(\frac{t-\tau_j-T_0}{P}\right)+\varepsilon ,
\end{equation}
where $s$ labels the survey segment (OGLE-II, -III, -IV, MACHO; separate zero points $z_s$), $j$ the observing season,
$\tau_j$ the season's delay, $\delta m_j$ a mean-magnitude offset and $\alpha_j$ an amplitude factor (both nuisance
parameters; $\alpha_j$ is also a Blazhko diagnostic), and $\varepsilon$ the photometric noise. A single $\tau_j$ shifts all
harmonics together: this is what a change in light-travel time does. The set $\{\tau_j\}$ versus time is the
\OC\ diagram; $\tau>0$ means the pulsation arrives late.

\subsection{Ordinary period change: the quadratic}
If the period changes slowly and steadily, $P(t)=P_0+\Pdot\,t$, the $n$-th maximum occurs at
$T_n=T_0'+nP_0+\tfrac12\Pdot P_0 n^2$, and with $n\simeq t/P_0$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quad}
\tau(t)=c_0+c_1\,t+c_2\,t^2,\qquad c_1=\frac{P_0-P}{P},\qquad c_2=\frac{\Pdot}{2P}.
\end{equation}
$c_0$ is an uninteresting constant (epoch and template-phase convention), $c_1$ a period error, $c_2$ the rate of
period change. For an evolutionary $\Pdot=0.1$\,d\,Myr$^{-1}$ ($2.7\times10^{-10}$\,d\,d$^{-1}$) and $P=0.55$\,d,
$c_2=2.5\times10^{-10}$\,d$^{-1}$, i.e.\ $\tau\approx2.5\times10^{-10}\times(4000)^2$\,d $=0.004$\,d $\approx350$\,s
at 4000\,d from the reference epoch: comparable to the orbital signals, so the quadratic is always part of the model.

\subsection{Light-travel-time effect}
If the RR Lyrae (mass $M_1$) orbits a companion ($M_2$), the delay is its line-of-sight position divided by $c$
\citep{Irwin1952}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ltte}
\tau_{\rm LTTE}(t)=\frac{a_1\sin i}{c}\;\frac{1-e^2}{1+e\cos\nu(t)}\,\sin\!\big(\nu(t)+\omega\big),
\end{equation}
with true anomaly $\nu$ from Kepler's equation, eccentricity $e$, argument of periastron $\omega$, and
\begin{equation}
a_1=\left[\frac{G(M_1+M_2)P_{\rm orb}^2}{4\pi^2}\right]^{1/3}\frac{M_2}{M_1+M_2}.
\end{equation}
From the fitted $P_{\rm orb}$ and semi-amplitude $A\equiv a_1\sin i/c$ one obtains the mass function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fm}
f(M_2)=\frac{4\pi^2(cA)^3}{G P_{\rm orb}^2}=\frac{(M_2\sin i)^3}{(M_1+M_2)^2},
\end{equation}
and the primary's radial-velocity semi-amplitude $K_1=2\pi c A/[P_{\rm orb}\sqrt{1-e^2}]$.
The full \OC\ model is $\tau(t)=c_0+c_1t+c_2t^2+\tau_{\rm LTTE}(t)$.

\subsection{Measuring one season's delay}
For fixed template $(a_k,b_k,z_s)$, each season's $(\tau_j,\delta m_j,\alpha_j)$ is fitted by weighted least squares
(Gauss--Newton after a coarse search over one full cycle). Its Fisher (Cram\'er--Rao) error is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fisher}
\sigma_{\tau_j}^{-2}\simeq\sum_{i\in j}\frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}\left(\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}\right)_i^2
=\sum_{i\in j}\frac{\alpha_j^2\,T'(\phi_i)^2}{P^2\sigma_i^2},
\end{equation}
scaled by $\sqrt{\max(\chi^2_\nu,1)}$. The steep rising branch dominates the sum. Template and delays are iterated to
convergence. Because a constant delay is degenerate with the template phase, we fix the gauge so that the
template's fundamental harmonic has phase zero.

\subsection{Detection statistic $D$}
On the season delays $\{t_j,\tau_j,\sigma_j\}$ we compare two Gaussian models with variance $\sigma_j^2+s^2$
(extra jitter $s$ fitted in each):
$H_0$: \cref{eq:quad} (+ a free MACHO offset); $H_1$: $H_0$ + $A_s\sin(2\pi t/P_{\rm orb})+A_c\cos(2\pi t/P_{\rm orb})$.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D}
D=2\max_{P_{\rm orb}}\left[\ln\mathcal L_1(P_{\rm orb})-\ln\mathcal L_0\right].
\end{equation}
At fixed $P_{\rm orb}$ and for white noise $D\sim\chi^2_2$; for $N$ seasons of error $\sigma$ and amplitude $A$,
$D\approx NA^2/(2\sigma^2)$. $D$ tests an orbit against a \emph{smooth} period change plus white noise; it does
not by itself reject irregular intrinsic period changes (\cref{sec:nuis}).

\section{The pipeline}\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Data}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{OGLE-IV} LMC RR Lyrae collection \citep{Soszynski2016}: 41{,}471 stars; $I$-band light curves for 41{,}209,
epochs HJD$'$ = 5260--8924, where HJD$'$ = HJD $-$ 2450000 (public data mostly end at 7507, i.e.\ 2016).
\url{https://www.astrouw.edu.pl/ogle/ogle4/OCVS/lmc/rrlyr/}
\item \textbf{OGLE-III} \citep{Soszynski2009}: 24{,}906 light curves, HJD$'=2166$--$4540$ (2001--2008); the 2024
re-issue of the files includes \textbf{OGLE-II} epochs (from HJD$'=447$, 1997) for 7{,}784 stars.
Same star numbering as OGLE-IV (24{,}818 matches, median separation $0.05''$).
\url{https://www.astrouw.edu.pl/ogle/ogle3/OIII-CVS/lmc/rrlyr/}
\item \textbf{MACHO} $B$ and $R$ photometry (1992--1999) via the NCI TAP service
(\url{https://machotap.asvo.nci.org.au/ncitap/tap}), queried per tile for the RR Lyrae only (MACHO IDs from the
OGLE-III cross-identification): 8{,}406 of 8{,}557 requested stars returned. We use MACHO $B$.
\item \textbf{Sample for this note:} 17{,}492 RRab with OGLE-III and OGLE-IV light curves; 6{,}614 with usable MACHO data;
2{,}378 of these also have OGLE-II (overlap years 1997--1999).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Time system}
OGLE times are HJD (UTC). MACHO \texttt{dateobs} is MJD and is converted to HJD for each star (observer at Mount
Stromlo); towards the LMC, near the south ecliptic pole, the heliocentric correction is only $\pm40$\,s
(tested). Whether MACHO times refer to the start or middle of the exposure is not documented; the band-lag test in
\cref{sec:tie} limits any residual MACHO--OGLE clock offset to $\approx\pm50$\,s.

\subsection{Seasons}
A season is one observing year, with the boundary at HJD$'$ mod 365.25 $=245$ (the middle of the OGLE seasonal gap).
MACHO observed the LMC nearly year-round, so gap-based seasons would merge years. Seasons with fewer than 15 epochs are dropped.

\subsection{Timing fit per star and band}
\cref{eq:lc} with $K=8$, separate zero points per survey segment, per-season $(\tau_j,\delta m_j,\alpha_j)$,
$4\sigma$ clipping, template$\leftrightarrow$delay iterations until the mask is stable and $\max|\Delta\tau|<10^{-7}$\,d,
the fundamental-phase gauge, and errors from \cref{eq:fisher} scaled by $\sqrt{\max(\chi^2_\nu,1)}$. Delays are
defined modulo $P$ and unwrapped between consecutive seasons.
\emph{Measured precision} (1000 random OGLE-III+IV RRab): median season error 253\,s (10--90\%: 121--510\,s), strongly
amplitude-dependent (476\,s for $A_I<0.4$, 113\,s for $A_I>0.8$); MACHO $B$ per year: median 135\,s.

\subsection{Tying MACHO to OGLE}\label{sec:tie}
Band-dependent template shapes leave a constant offset $\Delta$ between the MACHO $B$ and OGLE $I$ delay series
(\cref{fig:bands}). On 781 stars observed by both in 1997--1999: $\Delta/P=-0.034$ cycles (robust scatter 0.008), with
a $\sim300$-s star-to-star scatter. The same behaviour (lag $-0.035$ cycles, similar period dependence and scatter) is
found between OGLE's own $V$ and $I$, which share one time system: the offset is a physical band lag, not a MACHO clock
error. In the \OC\ search $\Delta$ is a free parameter per star (an indicator column); for stars with OGLE-II it is fixed
by the overlap years.

\subsection{Common-mode timing correction}
Stacking all stars' \OC\ residuals by year reveals a common MACHO pattern ($-113$ to $+40$\,s) and a smaller OGLE drift
($+10$ to $+24$\,s in 2006--2008). A null test (white-noise delays at the real epochs, same stacking) is consistent
with zero, so these are real timing systematics; the per-(survey, year) median is subtracted from every star (median
correction 12\,s rms per star; the candidate list is unchanged by it).

\subsection{Orbit search and the annual-sampling limit}\label{sec:nyquist}
$D$ (\cref{eq:D}) is maximized over trial periods uniform in frequency, from 800\,d to twice the baseline. Season
delays sample the \OC\ about once per year, so orbital periods below the Nyquist limit of 2\,yr are aliased: in an
earlier version the grid started at 300\,d and a 3000-d orbit could be assigned its alias at
$1/(1/365.25-1/3000)=416$\,d. All statistics in this note use the corrected grid.
Orbits shorter than 2\,yr would require within-season timing and are outside the present search.

\subsection{Nuisances and discriminators}\label{sec:nuis}
Simulated on each star's real cadence and errors from its own fitted templates (\texttt{src/rrlbin/simulate.py};
one delay realization shared by MACHO and OGLE): constant \Pdot; Blazhko (coherent phase and amplitude modulation,
$P_B=20$--3000\,d); abrupt period changes ($|\Delta P/P|$ from $10^{-6}$ to $2\times10^{-4}$); random-walk phase (50--15{,}000\,s rms);
LTTE (log-uniform $P_{\rm orb}=300$--$10^4$\,d, $M_2=0.05$--$1.5\,\Msun$, isotropic, half eccentric). Discriminators:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item amplitude constancy $\chi^2_\nu(\alpha_j)<2$ (Blazhko veto; LTTE does not change amplitude);
\item at least 1.5 orbital cycles within the baseline;
\item a physical ceiling: $A$ below $a_1/c$ for $M_2=2\,\Msun$ at the fitted period (costs 4.5\% of true LTTE; applied
identically to simulations);
\item for stars with OGLE-II: a predictive test (fit from 1997 on, predict the held-out 1992--1996 MACHO delays).
\end{itemize}
Real stars have a heavier tail of timing noise than the nuisances first simulated (jitter 90th/97th percentiles
1.6/3.8\,ks), which motivated the large random-walk and large-jump classes.

\subsection{Code and tests}
Package \texttt{rrlbin} (\texttt{io}, \texttt{timing}, \texttt{ltte}, \texttt{simulate}, \texttt{mixture}); 20 unit tests
(\texttt{pytest}): Fourier derivatives, exact recovery of noiseless delays ($<0.01$\,s), gauge invariance, pull
distributions of noisy delays, Kepler solver and eccentric delay amplitude, LTTE amplitudes of \cref{tab:amp}, mass
function, O$-$C search recovery and null calibration, the annual-Nyquist grid, predictive score, MJD$\to$HJD. Long
runs use a chunked, resumable runner. Full log: \texttt{JOURNAL.md}.

\subsection{The panels}\label{sec:panels}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,style=nextline]
\item[(a) Detection statistic.] Distribution of $D$ (\cref{eq:D}) for real stars (grey) and for four simulated classes.
White-noise nulls (black; only a constant \Pdot\ and the photometric noise) never exceed $D=23$ in 2{,}944
simulations; their per-star false-alarm thresholds are $D=12.4$ (5\%), 15.6 (1\%) and 20.2 (0.1\%). Real stars have
a long tail extending far beyond the nulls: at $D>40$ they number 691 (4.0\%) where white noise predicts none.
Simulated orbits (blue), Blazhko modulation (orange) and large abrupt period changes (green) all populate that tail,
so $D$ alone cannot separate orbits from intrinsic period changes.
\item[(b) $D$ versus best period.] Each star's $D$ at the period that maximizes it. The search starts at 800\,d
(\cref{sec:nyquist}); the vertical banding is the discreteness of the frequency grid (nearly all MACHO stars share an
8{,}350-d baseline and hence the same grid points). Candidates (red; 81\% of them) concentrate at 2{,}500--5{,}000\,d, the range in
which a 24-yr baseline covers about 2--4 cycles and the search is most sensitive.
\item[(c) Amplitude versus period.] Fitted semi-amplitude $A=a_1\sin i/c$ with the edge-on LTTE amplitude for
$M_2=0.15$, $0.5$ and $2\,\Msun$ ($M_1=0.65\,\Msun$). The solid line is the physical ceiling used in cut~5. The large cloud
above it at long periods consists of stars whose \OC\ changes by a large fraction of a pulsation cycle --- intrinsic
period changes far larger than any companion of $\le2\,\Msun$ can produce. Candidates lie between the $0.15$ and
$2\,\Msun$ curves, about half (52\%) above the $0.5\,\Msun$ curve.
\item[(d) Signal versus timing noise.] Orbit amplitude and extra jitter (fitted under $H_0$), both in units of the median
season error. Stars with no extra jitter under $H_0$ form the column at zero. A real orbit that is not modelled under
$H_0$ is absorbed as jitter, so strong orbits move up and to the right together; the discrete columns are the
jitter grid. Cut 3 (dashed) removes signals that are not resolved season by season.
\item[(e) Blazhko veto.] $\chi^2_\nu$ of the per-season amplitude factors $\alpha_j$ about their mean. A light-travel delay
shifts the light curve without changing its amplitude ($\chi^2_\nu\approx1$), whereas Blazhko modulation changes it:
the median $\chi^2_\nu$ is 0.97 for simulated orbits and 0.97 for nulls, but 1.8 for simulated Blazhko stars and 1.4
for real stars. The black histogram (real, $D>40$) is shifted to large $\chi^2_\nu$: most high-$D$ real stars change
amplitude from season to season.
\item[(f) Cycles covered.] Baseline divided by the best period. Real stars with long best periods ($<1.5$ cycles) are
numerous; at fewer than 1.5 cycles a sinusoid is not distinguishable from a smooth curve (cubic trend, broken line,
random walk).
\item[(g) Selection funnel.] Fraction of each class passing the cuts applied in sequence. Real stars (red) fall from
4.0\% to 0.39\%; simulated orbits (broad prior, blue) from 23\% to 13.6\%; the most orbit-like nuisance, large abrupt period
changes (green), from 64\% to 5.6\%; white-noise nulls pass none.
\item[(h) Candidates.] Minimum companion mass (edge-on, $M_1=0.65\,\Msun$, from \cref{eq:fm}) against orbital period,
coloured by $\log D$; dotted lines mark the three companion-mass groups suggested for the Galactic bulge by
\citet{Hajdu2021}. This panel is descriptive: which of these are binaries is not established (\cref{sec:status}).

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{$D>40$.} With 17{,}492 stars, fewer than one white-noise false alarm requires a per-star false-alarm
probability below $\sim6\times10^{-5}$, i.e.\ well beyond the largest null value ($D=23$ in 2{,}944 simulations).
$D>40$ is chosen conservatively high because the real contaminants are not white noise; lowering it mainly admits
more intrinsic period changes (panel a).
\item \textbf{Amplitude constancy, $\chi^2_\nu(\alpha)<2$.} Motivated by the physics: the LTTE is a pure time shift
(\cref{fig:lc}), so any season-to-season amplitude change points to Blazhko modulation (or changing blends). The cut
removes 80\% of simulated Blazhko stars that passed cut 1 (0.210 $\to$ 0.042) while keeping 99\% of the orbits
(0.230 $\to$ 0.227), and 56\% of real stars with $D>40$.
\item \textbf{$A/\sigma_{\rm season}>3$.} The orbital amplitude must be large compared with a single season's error, so
that the curve is seen season by season and is not only an integrated excess; this mainly guards against
low-amplitude, high-jitter solutions and has little effect after cuts 1--2.
\item \textbf{At least 1.5 orbital cycles.} The decisive cut against intrinsic period changes: a sinusoid fitted over
less than about one cycle mimics a smooth trend. It removes 89\% of the large abrupt period changes (0.604 $\to$
0.068) and 63\% of the large random walks, at the cost of a third of the simulated orbits --- those with periods longer than
about $2/3$ of the baseline, which cannot be confirmed as periodic anyway.
\item \textbf{Physical ceiling.} The fitted amplitude must be achievable with $M_2\le2\,\Msun$ at the fitted period
(panel c). At this stage it removes no simulated orbits (0.136 $\to$ 0.136) but most of the remaining large random walks
(0.023 $\to$ 0.005) and 19 real stars. Over the full sample 4.5\% of true orbits exceed the ceiling because of noise;
the cost is measured, and the cut is applied identically to data and simulations.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent The cuts were set on physical grounds and on the simulations, not tuned on the real candidates. They are not
a clean binary selection: large abrupt period changes still pass at 5.6\% and Blazhko at 2.5\% (\cref{tab:cuts}), and
their true incidence among LMC RRab is unknown.

\section{Part C: the candidates compared with the Galactic-bulge sample}\label{sec:hajdu}

This section uses the v3 pipeline (Part A of the refit: MACHO $R$, harmonic coherence, whole-cycle band alignment, band-lag
priors, smooth-wander noise model). Selection thresholds and contamination rates are \textbf{provisional} until the v3
simulations (Part B) are complete. Scripts: \texttt{scripts/partC\_*.py}, \texttt{compare\_hajdu.py}, \texttt{gaia\_timing*.py}.

\subsection{Our v3 candidates in brief}
The cuts of \cref{tab:cuts} applied to the v3 statistics select 75 stars (69 with MACHO). Each was fitted with a full Keplerian
LTTE orbit (Irwin delay + quadratic + MACHO offsets with priors + white jitter; multi-start least squares, residual bootstrap;
recovery of injected $e=0,0.4,0.7$ orbits verified). Independent of the detection statistic we examined: the per-season
amplitude constancy and harmonic coherence in every band, the out-of-sample prediction of the 1992--1996 MACHO seasons,
crowding indicators from Gaia DR3 (MACHO candidates are indistinguishable from their parent population; KS $p=0.2$--$0.99$),
and the Keplerian fit quality. Grouping:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Tier 1} (11): Keplerian fit not at the eccentricity bound and covering $\ge1.5$ cycles, $\chi^2_\nu<1.5$; amplitude and
coherence $\chi^2_\nu<1.5$; no crowding flag; predictive score $>2$. All have MACHO $B$, $R$ and OGLE $I$.
$P_{\rm orb}=5.4$--$14.5$\,yr, $e=0.15$--$0.57$, $M_{2,\min}=0.14$--$0.96\,\Msun$, $K_1=2.5$--$11.5\,\kms$, $V=19.2$--$19.7$.
\item \textbf{Tier 2} (17): at most one soft flag; predictive score $>0$ or unavailable. \textbf{Tier 3} (47): the rest.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The bulge sample of Hajdu et al.\ (2021)}
\citet{Hajdu2021} examined $\sim$27{,}500 OGLE-III/IV O$-$C diagrams of bulge RRab (data to 2019; mean/median baselines 10.5/7.4\,yr),
pre-selected $\sim$400 by eye, and retained 87 after MCMC LTTE fits including a period-change term, in three quality classes
(Q1: 25, $P_{\rm orb}$ uncertainty $<1\%$; Q2: 32; Q3: 30, problematic fits). Their Table 1 was parsed from the arXiv source
(\texttt{scripts/parse\_hajdu2021.py}).

\textbf{Follow-up status.} We found no published confirmation or refutation of any of the 87. A 2026 study co-authored by Hajdu
and Prudil states that ``only a single RRL is confirmed as belonging to a binary system'' \citep{Salinas2026}, namely
TU~UMa, a field star \citep{Liska2016}. Radial-velocity tests of Galactic field timing candidates have been largely negative
(15 of 19 without binarity, \citealt{Barnes2021}; KIC~2831097 ruled out, \citealt{Poretti2025}). The status of the bulge
candidates is therefore \emph{untested}, not refuted. (Literature search: \texttt{docs/reviews/hajdu\_followup.md}; an ADS
full-text query could not be run.)

\paragraph{Where the bulge sample is stronger: precision.} At $I\approx15$--16 the bulge stars are 3--4\,mag brighter; their
orbital parameters are 2--4 times more precise ($\sigma_A/A$ 1.7\% vs 6.7\%, $\sigma_P/P$ 0.5\% vs 1.1\%, $\sigma_e$ 0.02 vs 0.10
for Q1 vs Tier 1). Their seasons were also split into segments (more O$-$C points). Their posterior widths assume white timing
noise beyond a constant $\dot P$, which understates the uncertainty if the period wanders (\cref{sec:nuis}).

\paragraph{What our sample adds, and what it does not.}
The main way an RR~Lyrae imitates an orbit is intrinsic phase wander (irregular period changes are common in every population
studied; e.g.\ \citealt{Alcock2000} for LMC RRc). Our Tier~1 differ from the bulge candidates as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Independent instruments} (MACHO $B$, $R$ and OGLE $I$). This rules out \emph{instrumental} artefacts (time stamps,
photometric systematics), but \emph{not} intrinsic wander, which every telescope sees identically (the same-season timing residuals
of MACHO and OGLE are correlated, $\rho=0.37$, \cref{sec:nuis}).
\item \textbf{More cycles} (median 2.9 vs 1.9). This helps, but a smooth wander with a correlation time of a few years still
produces one or two bumps over 24\,yr, which a sinusoid plus a quadratic fits.
\item \textbf{An out-of-sample prediction passed} (1992--1996 MACHO seasons). A partial safeguard only: smooth wander can continue
smoothly into the held-out years (simulated wander passes the full Tier-1 proxy $\sim$1\% of the time).
\item \textbf{Calibrated contamination}: simulations with a noise model matched to the real stars. This is what shows that the
candidate \emph{number} is consistent with wander alone (Part B, \cref{sec:status}); the bulge analysis has no such calibration.
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{Where they are alike.} Our sensitivity limit ($A\gtrsim350$\,s) restricts us to the high-amplitude regime of the
bulge Q1 class: medians $A\approx800$\,s, $M_{2,\min}\approx0.45$--$0.5\,\Msun$, $K_1\approx6\,\kms$ in both
(\cref{fig:hajdu}a, f). We cannot test the low-mass Q2/Q3 population ($M_{2,\min}\sim0.1$--$0.2\,\Msun$).

\paragraph{Expectation.} The v3 simulations (Part B) show that the number of our candidates is consistent with the tail of the
real stars' own timing noise (69 observed vs $\sim$80 expected from noise at the candidate cuts; 24 vs $\sim$33 for a Tier-1 proxy).
Neither sample therefore establishes binarity statistically; the same is likely true of the bulge sample, whose selection was not
calibrated against intrinsic wander. What separates an orbit from wander is strict repetition over more cycles, i.e.\ more data. Confirmation is, however, harder: at $V\approx19.4$,
measuring $K_1\sim6\,\kms$ requires $\sim$1--2\,\kms\ per epoch after removing a $\sim$60\,\kms\ pulsation curve
(published LMC RR~Lyrae RVs reach 10--35\,\kms\ per epoch).

\subsection{A photometric test with Gaia}
Gaia DR3 epoch $G$ photometry (2014.6--2017.4) exists for 62 of the 75 candidates (median 34 usable transits, per-transit
S/N $\approx59$). We fitted a $G$-band template and two delays, one per half of the DR3 window; the unknown $G$--$I$ band lag
cancels in their difference $\Delta\tau$, which is compared with each Keplerian prediction.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Calibration on 228 quiet non-candidate stars} (Gaia $\Delta\tau$ vs OGLE's own $\Delta\tau$ at the same epochs, OGLE
errors included): robust scatter of $z$ = 1.64 (2.2\% beyond $5\sigma$) $\Rightarrow$ Gaia errors inflated by 1.64.
\item \textbf{DR3 result}: only 4 of 53 testable candidates are informative ($|\Delta\tau_{\rm pred}|>2\sigma$). Two agree
(16755, Tier~1, $z=0.8$; 13469, $z=0.3$); two disagree (13392, Tier~2, $z=-6.7$; 01106, Tier~3, $z=+3.5$), versus $\sim$0.5 expected
by chance from the control tail. DR3 is too sparse for most orbits.
\item \textbf{DR4 forecast} (expected December 2026; 2014.6--2020.1, \emph{assumed} $\sim$2$\times$ the DR3 epochs; bins 2014.6--2017.4
and 2017.4--2020.1, the latter entirely after the public OGLE-IV data): testable at $>2\sigma$ for 6 of 9 Tier~1 (5 at $>3\sigma$),
8 of 13 Tier~2, 13 of 31 Tier~3. DR4 can therefore confirm or reject most of the strongest candidates photometrically, at no
observing cost. OGLE-IV photometry after 2016 (not public) would be a stronger test still.
\end{itemize}
